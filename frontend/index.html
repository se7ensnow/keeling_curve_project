What the measurements show

Since March 1958, scientists at Mauna Loa Observatory in Hawaii have measured the carbon dioxide in the air every month. The station sits high on a volcano, far from cities and forests, so its air is a clean sample of the whole atmosphere. This page uses monthly measurements from to . Why one straight line is not enough

A straight line says every year adds the same amount of CO₂. The bars say otherwise: the early years add far less than the recent ones. That is the first clue that the trend is curved, which is what the curved models further down are built to capture.

The bars also bounce from year to year, between and ppm per year. Part of that is real year-to-year variation in the climate system, which none of these models try to predict.

Two motions in one curve: a steady rise and a yearly swing

Picture a child jumping up and down on an upward escalator. Their height is the steady climb of the escalator plus the jumping. CO₂ behaves the same way: a long-term rise (the escalator) with a repeating yearly swing on top (the jump). Pulling the two apart is the key to modelling the curve.

1. The escalator (trend)

Mostly the burning of fossil fuels and changes in land use carry the whole curve upward, by about ppm per year at the end of the record.

2. The jump (yearly swing)

Northern Hemisphere plants take CO₂ out of the air as they grow and give it back as leaves decay. The curve peaks in and is at its lowest in .

The same idea as a formula

Every model on this page is built from the same three parts:

CO₂(t) = Trend(t) + Seasonal(t) + ε(t)

Trend(t) is the slow rise, the escalator. Seasonal(t) is the yearly swing, the jump. ε(t) is the small leftover that no model captures. The models differ only in the shape they allow for each part.

Key takeaways

    How the yearly swing is modelled

    Any repeating curve can be built by stacking sine and cosine waves of different lengths, called harmonics. The curved models further down each use : a 12-month wave for the main shape, plus 6-, 4- and 3-month waves that sharpen it.

    A plain straight line has none, so it cannot follow the yearly swing at all. The whole swing is left over as error.

    Three models, one test

    Each model was fitted only on – . Everything from onward was held back, so the scores below measure how well each model predicts months it has never seen.

    Linear in what?

    1. Linear. A straight line, a + b·t. Least squares finds a and b in one step.

    2. Polynomial + seasonal. The curve bends, but every unknown number still just multiplies something known: t, t², a sine, a cosine. That makes it linear in its parameters, so one least-squares step still finds them all.

    3. Exponential + seasonal. The growth rate r sits inside e^(r·t), so no single step can find it. The model is truly non-linear in r. We try values of r and, for each one, solve the rest by least squares.

    Typical miss (RMSE)

    RMSE is the typical size of a miss, in ppm. Lower is better. A model that fits the months it saw but misses new ones has learned the past, not the pattern.
